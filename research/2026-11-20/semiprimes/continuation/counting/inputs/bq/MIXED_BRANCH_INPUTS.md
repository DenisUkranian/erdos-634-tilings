# Forced boundary in the adjacent, scale-one base-alpha family

Fix an integer $v\ge4$, put $u=v-1$, and set
$$
 a=v(v-1),\qquad b=2v-1,\qquad c=v^2,\qquad Q=b+c.
$$
The outer triangle has base angles $\alpha$, legs $bc$ and base $bQ$. The tile has sides $a,b,c$ opposite $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ and $3\alpha+2\beta=\pi$. This includes $b=p$, $Q=(p^2+6p+1)/4$ for the semiprime candidate. All claims below are necessary conditions and allow arbitrary T-junctions.

**Conclusion.** At each of the two base corners the tile has its $b$ edge on the base and its $c$ edge on the leg. Reading a leg from its base corner towards the apex, its entire sequence of side lengths is either $2v-1$ copies of $c$, or $v$ copies of $c$ followed by $v$ copies of $a$. The base has a run of at least $v$ consecutive $c$ edges. The possible counts $(X,Y,Z)$ of its $a,b,c$ edges are just
$$
(v,2v-1,v),\quad(v+1,v-1,v+1),\quad(0,2v-1,2v-1),\quad(1,v-1,2v).
$$
These conditions do not settle the existence of a tiling.

## Arithmetic of the two leg lengths

The primitive tile has $a+c=vb$ and $\gcd(v,b)=1$. In any leg decomposition $Xa+Yb+Zc=bc$, reduction modulo $b$ gives $X-Z=jb$ for an integer $j$. Hence $Y+vZ=c-ja$. If $j<0$ then $Z\ge b$; the leg length forces $Z=b$ and $X=Y=0$. If $j>0$, then $Y+vZ\le c-a=v$, so $Z\le1$. Lemma 31 of Beeson–Roşu excludes this. Its obtuse-angle, irrational-ratio and corner hypotheses hold: $2\cos\gamma=-u/v$ is rational and nonintegral, the base corners each contain exactly one $\alpha$, and the apex contains one $\alpha$ and two $\beta$. These angle counts follow from irrationality of $\alpha/\pi$ and $\beta=(\pi-3\alpha)/2$. Neither $a$ nor $b$ divides the other, and no board side is a single tile side. Therefore the other possible leg decompositions have
$$
  X=Z=i,\qquad Y=v(v-i),\qquad 2\le i\le v .
$$
In particular any leg with a $b$ edge has fewer than $v$ tiles with a $c$ edge on it.

## The two base corners

Suppose the tile at a base corner puts its $b$ edge on the adjacent leg. Apply Beeson–Roşu Lemma 34 to that leg. At the base corner its endpoint hypothesis holds vacuously, since the corner tile does not put its $c$ edge there. At the apex, if the tile adjacent to this leg shows $\alpha$ and puts its $c$ edge on the leg, the ray of its $b$ edge borders a tile showing $\beta$ and consequently placing its $a$ or $c$ edge on the ray. Thus the lemma forces at least $v$ consecutive $c$ edges. This contradicts the leg decomposition. Consequently both corner tiles put $b$ on the base and $c$ on their legs. Applying Lemma 34 to the base now gives the run on the base at once. All standing hypotheses of §11 hold as above.

For the base use again $X-Z=jb$ and $Y+vZ=Q-ja$. Since $Z\ge v$ we have $j\le0$ ($Q-a=3v-1<v^2$). Also $Z\le\lfloor bQ/c\rfloor<2b$, so $j\ge-1$. When $j=0$, nonnegativity and $Z\ge v$ give $Z=v$ or $v+1$. When $j=-1$, $Z=X+b$ and $Y+vX=b$ force $X=0$ or $1$. Substitution gives the four stated base counts.

## Why both legs have a long run as well

We use the external-wall corner argument of Bonfioli’s row lemma in the Beeson–Roşu angular family. Bonfioli proves the corner version in a different angular family, with the same pure-edge rigidity windows and the same local wall on the exterior side. [Bonfioli, row lemma (K) and its detailed proof](https://github.com/ElVec1o/erdos634_primes_and_N200/blob/752005605ca4e607d2a08f57bdc2445dd5558dbb/paper/erdos634_primes_and_N200.tex#L488-L520).

Here is the endpoint variant of Beeson–Roşu Lemma 33 used below. Retain its hypotheses of a run of length $n<v$, stopped at its far end $V$, whose first tile has its $\alpha$ angle at $U$ and $b$ edge on the ray $UW$. Suppose instead of an adjacent tile across $UW$ that $U$ is a corner, and $UW$ follows a board side for length strictly greater than $(v-1)b$. This configuration also cannot occur.

Here are the details of the change to the proof. In its induction $H(w)$, a quadratically tiled corner patch of order $w$ has vertices $U$, $P=x(0,w)$ on $UW$ and $Q'=x(w,0)$ on the side supporting the run. The only use of the adjacent tile across $UW$ in the original proof is to get the continuing wall beyond $P$ (the paragraph headed “The line $UP$”). Under the boundary hypothesis $P$ lies in the strict relative interior of the board side, since $UP=wb\le nb\le(v-1)b$. The board side itself supplies the continuing ray. The segment $PQ'$ starts at this boundary and is not parallel to it, so no tile edge on its line extends through $P$ in the opposite direction. A tile corner $\gamma$ of the patch at $P$ leaves exactly $\pi-\gamma=\alpha+\beta$ on the board side of that line beyond the patch, as in the original induction.

At $Q'$ the supporting boundary and the stopped-run hypothesis are unchanged. Thus the opposite bank partitions $PQ'$ into $w$ edges of length $a$ by Lemma 30(A). Its boundary tiles are the forced half-turns identified in Lemma 33, since no $\gamma$ sits in the $\alpha+\beta$ sector at $P$ and no two $\gamma$ sit in a half-plane. If $w=n$ this puts a forbidden $\gamma$ at the stop. Otherwise, at the other end of the new row the remaining sector along the continued exterior ray at $P$ is $\pi-\gamma-\beta=\alpha$. At each exposed vertex the next tile therefore has the required orientation or supplies alternative (ii) of Lemma 32; the latter is impossible by the same first-deviation argument, with $s=w+1\le n<v$. This is exactly the induction from $H(w)$ to $H(w+1)$, starting at $H(1)$. It yields the contradiction at the stop. In this boundary version the $\pi$-sector east of line $UP$ is supplied by the board itself throughout the induction.

Now apply the counting proof of Lemma 34 to a leg. It finds a maximal $c$-run stopped at both ends. If its length is $<v$, its proof by Lemmas 32–33 handles every case except a start at the outer base corner with a $c$ edge on the leg. The boundary variant just proved also handles that start, since the $b$ ray runs along the base of length $bQ>(v-1)b$. The endpoint condition at the apex was verified above. Hence the leg supports a run of length at least $v$. The arithmetic leaves only the pure-$c$ leg or $i=v$. In the latter case there are exactly $v$ edges of type $c$; they form a single run, starting at the base corner because its tile puts a $c$ edge on the leg. All $v$ subsequent edges are $a$.

## At least one leg is entirely made of c edges

When the leg word is mixed, its initial run of $v$ edges $c$ is stopped at the far end. Indeed, all $v$ succeeding $a$ edges place one $\gamma$ each along this last portion of the leg. The apex cannot carry $\gamma$ and no two $\gamma$ can occupy an internal point of that portion. Reading backwards from the apex therefore forces every one of these $a$ tiles to show $\gamma$ at its endpoint nearer the base.

Beeson–Roşu Lemma 32 remains valid when its upper limit on the stopped run is $n=v$. To check the endpoint, take its maximal-counterexample proof without changing the range $1\le r<s\le n$. The only two numerical rigidity calls use Lemma 30(C) on $(s-r-i_0)c$ and Lemma 30(A) on $ra$. Both coefficients are strictly less than $v$ even for $s=n=v$, since $r\ge1$ and $r<s$. The stopping and maximality steps use $s<n$ before increasing $s$. The nonnumeric parts of this argument do not change.

In the boundary-wall induction for a mixed leg, this extended trapezoid lemma permits every step $H(w)\implies H(w+1)$ for $1\le w<v$. In these steps the entire diagonal has length $wa$ with $w<v$ and the other side continues beyond $wb$, so the required full-column rigidity holds. It follows that the $v^2$-tile quadratic triangular patch at this base corner is forced. One side of the patch consists of $v$ consecutive $b$ edges on the outer base, starting at that corner. Its other external side consists of the initial $v$ edges $c$ on the leg. Applying this to two mixed legs would require at least $2v$ edges of length $b$ on the base. The two corner portions are disjoint since the base length $bQ$ exceeds $2vb$. All four base rows above have at most $2v-1$ such edges. Thus at least one whole leg is $c^{2v-1}$. In the case of exactly one mixed leg the base rows reduce to $(v,2v-1,v)$ and $(0,2v-1,2v-1)$.

The short-run wall argument also has a general consequence before the specialization $u=v-1$. For any $0<u<v$ with $\gcd(u,v)=1$, and any positive integer $g$, all three sides of a base-$\alpha$ target with legs $gbc$ and base $gb(b+c)$ support at least $v$ consecutive $c$ edges. Each base corner has one $\alpha$ and the apex has one $\alpha$ and two $\beta$. If an incident tile shows $\alpha$ and puts $c$ on a tested side, its $b$ ray either borders a $\beta$ tile (which puts $a$ or $c$ there) or is on the other exterior side of length at least $gbc>(v-1)b$. Thus the two endpoint versions cover every side. Exact lengths and their order remain a further restriction special to the adjacent scale-one case.

As checks on the arithmetic, direct enumeration of the length equations with $Z\ge v$ gives the stated two leg inventories and four base inventories for all $4\le v\le220$. In the known 280-tile dissection, which has $(u,v,g)=(2,3,2)$, the longest $c$ runs on its three sides have lengths $4,4,5$, as predicted by the general bound. ## The order of the three tiles at the apex

The order is $\beta,\alpha,\beta$ from one leg to the other. For this conclusion we allow another type of stop in the preceding induction. Suppose a block of $h<v$ $c$ edges starts in the protected $\alpha$ orientation and ends at a boundary junction before a $c$ tile facing the opposite way. If this is the *first* reversal, the two boundary tiles each show $\beta$ at the junction. The remaining sector is $\pi-2\beta=3\alpha$ and consists only of three $\alpha$ corners by irrationality. This junction acts as a stop. At the terminal case $s=n=h$ in Lemma 32, the edge of the first tile in the residual sector along the diagonal must be $b$ or $c$, contrary to the required pure $a$ decomposition of its segment of length $ra$ with $r<v$. All cases $s<n$ are unchanged. At the terminal case $w=n=h$ in Lemma 33, the forced inverted tile would place $\gamma$ at this junction, which has no $\gamma$ in its residual sector. This proves that the first $v$ tiles of an all-$c$ leg, read from a protected $\alpha$ end, must show their $\alpha$ toward that end. Both endpoint hypotheses used above are available, one by the external wall and one by the adjacent $\beta$ tile at the apex if it were an $\alpha$ end. An all-$c$ leg has $2v-1$ tiles. If both ends showed $\alpha$, the two forced length-$v$ prefixes would overlap, requiring its middle tile to have $\alpha$ at both ends of its $c$ edge. Hence a pure leg has $\beta$ at the apex. The mixed leg does also (its terminal $a$ tile cannot show $\gamma$ there). This gives the stated apex order.

A mixed leg has its initial $v$ $c$-tiles oriented with $\alpha$ toward the base, by its forced quadratic corner patch. Its last $v$ $a$-tiles have $\beta$ facing the apex, by the same obtuse-angle counting that stopped the run. In particular all placements along a mixed leg are determined once that leg is chosen.

## Orientation on a pure leg

Every tile on a pure $c$ leg faces its $\alpha$ angle toward the base corner. The preceding argument gives the first $v$ tiles in this orientation from the base. From the apex, the first tile shows $\beta$ on that same leg, and its $a$ ray borders the unique middle $\alpha$ tile. The middle tile puts $b$ or $c$ along that ray. We claim that the first $v-1$ tiles from the apex show $\beta$ toward the apex. These two prefixes together account for every one of the $2v-1$ tiles and give the same orientation.

For the claim, apply the short-row induction with the two acute-angle labels interchanged. The actual hypotheses in the trapezoid and row inductions (Lemmas 32–33) are symmetric geometric facts about the two acute corners: each single acute angle is uniquely filled by a tile with that angle, a sector of size $\alpha+\beta$ has one angle of each kind and no $\gamma$, and at most one $\gamma$ lies in any half-plane. These statements hold here by irrationality and $\gamma>\pi/2$. The metric inputs after interchanging labels are the purity of $na$ and $nb$, and that $nc$ contains no $a$, for $1\le n<v-1$. The purity statements follow from Lemma 30(A,B). For the last assertion, (C) excludes $b$, and any decomposition $nc=Xa+Zc$ with $X>0$ forces $v\mid X$ and $n-Z\ge v-1$. These are precisely the three rigidity inputs used at the upper clamp, the diagonal, and the start.

If the orientation first reverses after $h<v-1$ steps, the two boundary angles at the joint are now $\alpha,\alpha$. The residual sector $\pi-2\alpha=\alpha+2\beta$ contains no $\gamma$; this follows from the independent angle coefficients and $\gamma=2\alpha+\beta$. In the trapezoid proof, terminal case $s=n=h$, there is a clamp at the bottom since the two boundary tiles have a vertex at this joint. The opposite bank of the diagonal is therefore pure by the diagonal rigidity bound. Its angles are forced from the top of the diagonal downwards by the at-most-one-$\gamma$ bound, so its bottom tile would put $\gamma$ at the joint, exactly as in the original proof for a terminal vertex. This is impossible in its $\gamma$-free residual sector. The same top-down bank argument in the terminal full-row case $w=h$ puts a $\gamma$ at that joint. All earlier steps are the ordinary row induction. Hence a first reversal at $h<v-1$ is impossible.

This specifies, up to interchanging the legs, the placements of *every boundary tile along both equal sides*. There are only two cases, two pure legs or one pure and one mixed leg. The base order and the possible fillings of the remaining interior are still undetermined.

**The mixed-leg cut has two exact banks.** On the diagonal of the forced quadratic corner patch there are $v$ whole $a$ edges on the patch side and $v-1$ whole $c$ edges on the other side. The diagonal joins strict interior points of the outer leg and base, so every tile meeting its opposite bank along an interval has a whole side there. At its endpoint on the leg, the corner patch shows $\beta$ and the succeeding outer $a$ tile shows $\gamma$, leaving a unique $\alpha$ tile. Consequently the side starting the opposite bank is $b$ or $c$. Its total length is $va$. To see all its edges are $c$, write $Xa+Yb+Zc=va$. Reducing modulo $v$ gives $Y=vt$. After division by $v$,
$$
X(v-1)+t(2v-1)+Zv=v(v-1).
$$
If $t>0$ then $t<v$ and $X\equiv -t\pmod v$, whence $X\ge v-t$ and the left-hand side exceeds the right-hand side. Thus $Y=0$, and either $X=v,Z=0$ or $X=0,Z=v-1$. The first option has been ruled out by the endpoint angle. The cut therefore contributes a fully specified interior row of support intervals as well as the exterior boundary data.

**An exact limitation of apex-only tests.** The pure-leg choice has an infinite extension at the apex for every $v$. Split the infinite cone of angle $\alpha+2\beta$ into successive sectors $\beta,\alpha,\beta$. In each outer sector put a copy of the tile with its $\beta$ angle at the cone point and its $c$ edge along the exterior ray. In the central sector put the $\alpha$ angle of an arbitrary correctly oriented copy at the cone point. Any one sector spanned by the two side vectors incident to its chosen tile corner is tiled by the ordinary grid of parallelograms generated by those two vectors, cut along their tile diagonals. The grids from adjacent sectors meet on their common ray, and their possibly different subdivision lengths are permitted. This produces the prescribed pure-leg rows and apex fan simultaneously for arbitrarily long legs. A proof excluding the finite board in this case must use a condition such as the opposite base, the finite scale, or a global area or edge balance.
