# How many primitive F2 boards can have a prescribed odd factor?

Let an ordered primitive board be specified by positive coprime integers $a,b$ with $c^2=a^2+ab+b^2$, $c>0$. The two F2 factors are $a+2b$ and $2a+b$. Both orders of the legs are available. No assertion about a dissection is part of this definition.

**Theorem.** Fix any odd integer $M>1$. If every prime divisor of $M$ is $1$ or $11$ modulo $12$, there are exactly $2^{\omega(M)-1}$ ordered primitive boards with $a+2b=M$, where $\omega$ counts distinct prime divisors. Otherwise there are none. Distinct boards give distinct partners $2a+b$.

In particular, an odd prime has at most one F2 partner of any kind. Hence the graph of distinct prime pairs that pass the F2 arithmetic test is a matching. A given prime cannot have one such partner above itself and another below. A prime's unique partner may be composite or even. Existence of the corresponding scale-one tiling still requires geometry.

## Reduction to a norm

Write $R=\mathbb Z[\sqrt3]$, $x=2c$, $y=a$, and $z=x+y\sqrt3$. The board conditions imply
$$
x^2-3y^2=M^2,\quad 0<y<M,\quad x>0\text{ even},\quad y\text{ odd},\quad \gcd(y,M)=1.
$$
Conversely these conditions recover the board by $a=y$, $b=(M-y)/2$, $c=x/2$. Its partner is $(M+3y)/2$. The equivalence uses that $M$ is odd. If $3\mid M$, then $3\mid x$ and the norm equation modulo $9$ forces $3\mid y$, violating primitivity. If $r>3$ divides $M$, then $(x/y)^2=3$ in $\mathbb F_r$. Quadratic reciprocity gives exactly the allowed residue classes $r\equiv\pm1\pmod{12}$.

## All representations at a split modulus

The ring $R$ is Euclidean for the absolute norm. Round each rational coefficient of a quotient in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt3)$ to the nearest integer. For the resulting errors $h,k$ one has $|h^2-3k^2|\le3/4<1$. This proves the division property, hence unique factorization. At each allowed prime $r$, the quotient $R/(r)$ is $\mathbb F_r\times\mathbb F_r$ by its two roots of 3. The two kernels are conjugate principal prime ideals of index $r$. Multiplication by $d+e\sqrt3$ has determinant $d^2-3e^2$, so each generator $\pi_r$ has absolute norm $r$. In particular $r$ is associated to $\pi_r\bar\pi_r$ and the two factors are nonassociate.

For $r^e\parallel M$, the $r$-factors of $z$ of norm $M^2$ must give total exponent $2e$. They all go on one side of the conjugate pair. Taking both would make $r$ divide $x$ and $y$. Conversely, in any assignment of all $2e$ to one side at every prime, no $r\mid M$ divides both coefficients. For an element of norm $M^2$, if $r\mid y$ then $r\mid x$, so the required primitivity follows. Every assignment gives a representative obtained by multiplying the chosen prime powers. Their exponents are even, so the norm is positive and both real embeddings are positive. There are $2^{\omega(M)}$ assignments.

Multiplication by the totally positive units preserves these assignments. Their group is generated by $\epsilon=2+\sqrt3$. Indeed, a norm-one unit $X+Y\sqrt3>1$ with positive conjugate has $Y\ge1$ and $X\ge2$. Hence $\epsilon$ is the smallest such unit and division by a suitable power gives the assertion. Representatives of a given assignment that are positive in both embeddings differ by such a unit. Normalize each by powers of $\epsilon$ so that
$$
1<z/M<\epsilon .
$$
There is no endpoint because $z=M$ up to a unit would have divided its prime exponents equally between conjugates. For a normalized $z$, one has $0<y<M$ since $y/M=((z/M)-(M/z))/(2\sqrt3)$ increases from 0 to 1 on this interval.

Conjugation interchanges assignments without a fixed assignment. The normalized representative of the conjugate assignment is $\epsilon\bar z$, whose coefficients are $(2x-3y,x-2y)$. Since $M$ is odd, $x,y$ have opposite parity. The displayed transformation exchanges their parities, so precisely one representative in each conjugate pair has even $x$ and odd $y$. This proves the formula, including positivity and primitivity. Distinct $y$ give distinct partners, and a fixed $y$ uniquely determines the positive $x$.

## Replay and examples

The generator computes a modular square root at each split prime, takes a Euclidean gcd in $R$, forms the prime powers, then normalizes with integer arithmetic. A separate loop checks the defining square equation directly for every admissible $b$.

```sh
python3 all_factors.py --verify-through 5001 11 13 23 47 61 73 83 121 143
```

For $M=23$ the sole board $(7,8,13)$ has partner 22. For $M=143$ the two primitive boards $(17,63,73)$ and $(95,24,109)$ have partners 97 and 214. For the controls 47 and 61 the partners are respectively 73 and 83. Starting at either of the latter two primes gives the same board with the legs exchanged.
